## Purpose

This page walks through one pass of the DevOS workflow: describing an idea, completing the
product interview, reviewing scored recommendations, and accepting decisions into the ledger.
Expect 30 to 60 minutes for a first project.

## Before you start

You need:

- A DevOS account with a **Contributor** or **Owner** role in an organisation. A **Viewer**
  can read a project but cannot accept decisions. See
  [Roles and workspaces](/getting-started/roles-and-workspaces).
- A rough idea of what you want to build. Two or three sentences is enough; the interview
  extracts the rest.
- Answers to, or an honest "I don't know" for, the constraint questions below.

You do **not** need a finished specification. Arriving with a fully-formed spec defeats the
purpose of the interview, which is to surface the assumptions inside it.

### Constraints worth knowing in advance

The interview will ask about these. "I don't know" is a valid, recorded answer — it becomes
an evidence gap that lowers confidence rather than a blank the system fills in silently.

- Who the users are, and what job they are hiring the software to do.
- Whether you handle regulated data (payments, health records, personal data at scale).
- Expected concurrency or transaction volume at launch and at 12 months.
- Hard integration requirements — systems you must talk to.
- Team size and the languages the team already knows.
- Whether there is a delivery deadline that outranks technical preference.

## Step 1 — Create a project and describe the idea

Create a project in your customer workspace and enter the idea in plain language.

```text
A scheduling tool for independent physiotherapy clinics. Clinic staff book
appointments, patients confirm by SMS, and clinicians see a daily list with
treatment notes. Small clinics, two to fifteen staff. UK-based.
```

Write what you actually intend, including what you are unsure about. The interview branches
on this text, so hedging produces a worse interview than admitting uncertainty.

## Step 2 — Complete the product interview

The interview adapts to your answers rather than walking a fixed questionnaire. The example
above triggers a health-data branch because of "treatment notes", and an SMS-integration
branch because of patient confirmation.

Expect roughly 15 to 30 questions across six areas:

| Area | What it establishes |
| --- | --- |
| Users and jobs | Who acts in the system and what outcome they need |
| Functional scope | What the system must do, and explicitly must not do |
| Non-functional constraints | Scale, latency, availability, cost ceilings |
| Compliance | Regulated data classes and jurisdiction |
| Integrations | External systems that are non-negotiable |
| Team and delivery | Skills available, deadline pressure |

Two rules make the output useful:

1. **Answer "I don't know" when you don't know.** The brief records unknowns as unknowns.
   A guess entered as fact produces a high-confidence recommendation built on nothing.
2. **Name the non-goals.** What you are deliberately not building constrains the
   architecture as much as what you are.

The interview produces a **Product Brief** — the structured input to every later stage.
Review it before continuing. Correcting the brief now is cheap; correcting it after
decisions are accepted requires supersession.

See [Product Interview Engine](/engines/product-interview-engine).

## Step 3 — Review architecture recommendations

DevOS matches the brief against the [Framework Library](/frameworks/overview) and produces
several candidate architectures. You get candidates rather than a single answer because a
brief containing genuine ambiguity should not yield false certainty.

Each recommendation shows:

- The framework it derives from, and why it matched.
- A **confidence score** from 0.00 to 1.00.
- The evidence supporting the score.
- The **evidence gaps** that would move it.

Read the gaps first. A recommendation at 0.45 with the gap "expected concurrency unknown" is
telling you exactly what to go and find out. Supplying that answer and re-running is usually
better than accepting a low-confidence recommendation.

See [Decision Engine](/engines/decision-engine) and
[Architecture Confidence Engine](/engines/architecture-confidence-engine).

## Step 4 — Confidence review

Work through the recommendations deliberately. For each one, decide:

- **Accept** — the reasoning holds and the confidence is adequate for this choice.
- **Reject** — record why. Rejections are kept; the reasoning is the value.
- **Defer** — the evidence gap is worth closing before deciding.

Nothing enters the ledger without a human accepting it. DevOS does not auto-accept, even at
high confidence — see [Philosophy](/introduction/philosophy).

## Step 5 — Accept decisions into the ledger

Accepted recommendations become **decision records**: context, decision, alternatives
considered, consequences, and the confidence at the time of acceptance.

Records are immutable. When a decision changes later, the original is marked superseded and
points at its replacement, so the history of the project's architecture stays legible.

At this point you have the durable output of the current DevOS implementation: a structured
brief and a ledger that explains the architecture and the reasoning behind it.

See [Decision Ledger](/engines/decision-ledger).

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Entering a polished spec instead of an idea | The interview validates your assumptions rather than testing them | Describe the problem, let the interview extract the spec |
| Guessing at constraints to avoid "I don't know" | High confidence resting on invented evidence | Record the unknown; close the gap deliberately |
| Accepting every recommendation in one pass | A ledger full of choices nobody actually weighed | Accept, reject, or defer each one on its merits |
| Ignoring evidence gaps | Reversals later, for reasons that were visible now | Close the highest-impact gap and re-run |
| Skipping non-goals | An architecture scoped for work you will never do | State what you are not building |
